Gravitational Waves

Two massive objects orbiting each other do not simply move through space — they warp it. General relativity tells us mass curves the geometry of spacetime itself, and as these objects accelerate through their orbit, they continuously distort the fabric around them. This simulation visualizes that curvature: the grid you see is not decoration but a representation of spacetime geometry, deforming in real time as the two black holes spiral inward under their mutual gravity.

The Chirp

As the orbit decays, gravitational wave frequency rises — slowly at first, then explosively in the final moments before merger. This is the chirp: a sweep from near-silence to a sharp crescendo lasting fractions of a second. The higher the frequency, the faster it rises — a runaway acceleration ending only at collision. LIGO detected exactly this signature on September 14 2015, confirming a century-old prediction in 0.2 seconds of signal.

The Machine That Heard Space

LIGO splits a single laser beam down two perpendicular 4km arms and measures whether one arm stretches or compresses relative to the other. A passing gravitational wave changes this distance by less than one ten-thousandth the diameter of a proton. The first detection GW150914 came from two black holes 1.3 billion light years away. In the fraction of a second before they merged, they radiated more power than every star in the observable universe combined.
